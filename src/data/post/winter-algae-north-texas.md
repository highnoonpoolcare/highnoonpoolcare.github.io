Most pool owners relax completely about algae once the water gets cold. In a genuinely northern climate that is reasonable. Here it is optimistic.

## Our winters are not consistently cold

This is the crux of it. North Texas winters are interrupted. We get a hard freeze, then a stretch of days in the sixties and seventies, then another cold snap.

Those warm stretches are enough to wake algae up, especially in a pool where chlorine has been allowed to drift to nothing because it is December and nobody is thinking about it.

## What winter algae looks like

It usually starts slower and subtler than a summer bloom. Rather than the pool turning green over a weekend, you get:

- A slightly dull cast to the water that is easy to attribute to weather
- Faint discoloration on steps or in corners where circulation is weakest
- Surfaces that feel slick before anything is visible
- Chlorine that will not hold, dropping to zero between tests

That last one is the reliable early warning. If chlorine keeps disappearing in cold water with nobody swimming, something is consuming it.

## Why it is harder to fix in winter

Here is the part that catches people out. Algae grows more slowly in cold water, but it also _dies_ more slowly.

Chemical reactions are slower across the board at low temperature. A shock treatment that would clear a summer pool in two days may take the better part of a week in January. The filter also has to remove the dead algae, and if you cut pump run time back for winter, you have less turnover to do it with.

Prevention is dramatically cheaper than correction, and the gap is wider in winter than in summer.

## Keeping it from starting

**Do not let chlorine reach zero.** A modest baseline maintained all winter is the entire defense.

**Keep pH in range.** High pH makes your chlorine ineffective regardless of the level.

**Keep circulating daily.** Dead spots are where winter algae begins, typically on steps and in corners.

**Brush occasionally.** Even a quick brush every few weeks disrupts growth before it establishes.

**Keep debris out.** Decaying leaves consume chlorine and feed algae at the same time.

None of that is much work. It is perhaps fifteen minutes every couple of weeks, and it is the difference between a straightforward spring and an expensive one.
