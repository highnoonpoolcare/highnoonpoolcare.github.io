Most pool owners learn about chlorine and pH quickly. Calcium hardness takes longer to show up on the radar, and in Denton County it is arguably the more important number.

## Why it climbs here

Municipal water across North Texas comes in hard. That is just the source water.

Now add a summer of evaporation. Every time water evaporates, the calcium stays behind. Every time you top off, you add more. Over a hot season with constant topping off, hardness concentrates steadily, and by October a pool can be well above where it started in April.

Nothing removes it except dilution. Calcium does not burn off, filter out, or evaporate.

## What high hardness actually does

**Scale on tile and surfaces.** The chalky white line at the waterline is the visible version. It is unattractive and, once established, takes real effort to remove.

**Scale inside equipment.** This is the expensive version. Calcium deposits inside a heater reduce efficiency and eventually damage the heat exchanger. On a salt system, scale on the cell shortens its life considerably, and cells are not cheap.

**Cloudy water that will not clear.** Very high hardness can cause persistent cloudiness that people chase with clarifier for weeks without success.

## And low hardness is not better

If hardness runs too low, the water becomes aggressive and pulls calcium out of whatever it can reach, which means your plaster. Etching and pitting follow. This is more common in pools that were recently refilled with softened water.

Both directions cause damage. The goal is a range, not a minimum.

## What to actually do

**Test it, at least monthly.** Test strips are not precise enough for hardness. This is one where a proper test kit or a professional test matters.

**Manage pH and alkalinity alongside it.** Scale forms most readily when hardness is high _and_ pH is high. Holding pH in range buys you meaningful tolerance on hardness.

**Partial drain and refill.** When hardness gets genuinely high, the only real fix is replacing some water. Draining a third and refilling is a common approach here, usually best done in late fall or winter when evaporation will not immediately undo it.

A word of caution: never fully drain a pool in North Texas clay without knowing what you are doing. The soil movement risk is real and an empty shell can shift or pop.
